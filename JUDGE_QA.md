# Judge Q&A Preparation

This document prepares the team for likely judge questions based on the actual results of the pipeline.

## A. RESEARCH QUESTION

**Q: Why did you choose this problem?**
A: Diversification is the cornerstone of risk management, but it famously breaks down during severe market stress. We wanted to investigate whether this breakdown (tail dependence) is sensitive to the investment horizon. If short-term crashes behave differently than long-term market shifts, static risk models will misprice portfolio risk.

**Q: What does the wavelet component add?**
A: Standard correlation or copula models mix all frequencies of data together. Wavelets (specifically MODWT) allow us to cleanly separate the daily returns into distinct frequency bands (e.g., 2-4 days, 8-16 days) without losing temporal alignment, letting us measure dependence at specific horizons.

**Q: What does the copula component add?**
A: Copulas isolate the dependence structure from the marginal distributions. Linear correlation cannot capture asymmetric tail behavior (the tendency of assets to crash together more violently than they rally together). Copulas allow us to formally model and simulate joint extreme risks.

## B. PORTFOLIO

**Q: Why SPY, TLT and GLD?**
A: We selected a 60/30/10 portfolio because it represents a classic, economically interpretable multi-asset strategy. SPY drives growth, TLT provides duration and a traditional deflationary hedge, and GLD acts as an alternative safe-haven/inflation hedge. We wanted to see if the tail dependence between these theoretically diversifying assets changes across horizons.

## C. DATA

**Q: What is the exact sample?**
A: We used daily adjusted close prices from Yahoo Finance from 2012-01-01 to 2026-10-06. We trained the model on 3,017 observations up to 2023-12-31 and left 692 observations for out-of-sample testing. 

## D. TRAINING / TESTING

**Q: How did you prevent leakage?**
A: The threshold parameters, marginal ECDFs, wavelet decomposition arrays, and copula parameters were fitted strictly on the data up to 2023-12-31. For the out-of-sample test, we applied the fixed 99% VaR and ES thresholds generated by the trained model to the realized portfolio returns from 2024 onwards. No rolling estimation was used.

## E. WAVELETS

**Q: Why db4 and these horizons?**
A: Daubechies 4 (db4) provides a good balance between time and frequency localization for financial data. Level 1 details represent roughly 2-4 day dynamics (short-term trading shocks), Level 2 captures 4-8 days, and Level 3 captures 8-16 days (medium-term structural shifts). The remaining approximation captures the long-term trend.

## F. COPULAS

**Q: Why Gaussian and Student-t?**
A: We evaluated Gaussian and Student-t for the 3D portfolio simulation. Gaussian copulas have zero asymptotic tail dependence, while Student-t copulas can model joint heavy tails. We used AIC to select the best fit. (We avoided 3D Archimedean copulas like Clayton because they impose overly strict symmetry assumptions on a multi-asset portfolio).

**Q: Are your pairwise copulas jointly coherent?**
A: Yes, for the portfolio risk simulation, we use a true 3D multivariate copula (e.g., 3D Student-t), ensuring mathematical coherence. We only use pairwise empirical estimates to analyze specific bivariate tail dependence relationships for our charts.

## G. TAIL DEPENDENCE

**Q: Are differences across horizons statistically meaningful?**
A: We see strong empirical differences. For example, SPY-GLD lower tail dependence at the 5% threshold is 0.086 at the short horizon but doubles to 0.180 at the long horizon. However, as the horizon lengthens and we isolate specific frequencies, the effective sample size of independent tail events decreases, increasing estimation uncertainty.

## H. VaR & EXPECTED SHORTFALL

**Q: What does VaR actually mean?**
A: A 99% VaR of -1.8% means that, assuming the model is correct, we expect the portfolio to lose more than 1.8% on only 1 out of every 100 trading days. 

**Q: Why ES in addition to VaR?**
A: VaR only tells us the threshold of the tail. It does not tell us how bad things get once we cross that threshold. Expected Shortfall (ES) calculates the average loss beyond the VaR threshold, capturing the severity of extreme events.

## J. BENCHMARK & OUT-OF-SAMPLE RESULTS

**Q: What is your benchmark and how did you perform?**
A: Our benchmark is a standard Historical Simulation VaR/ES. In our out-of-sample test of 692 days, we expected roughly 7 violations for a 99% VaR. The Historical benchmark had 5 violations, which is excellent (Kupiec p-value = 0.44). 

Our proposed Wavelet-Copula model had 28 violations. It severely underestimated risk.

**Q: Why should we believe your model is better if it failed the OOS test?**
A: We do *not* claim the proposed model is better for calculating aggregate VaR. We claim the proposed model *successfully answers the research question* by proving that treating different time horizons as statistically independent destroys the cross-horizon volatility clustering present in real market crashes. The failure of the model is the exact evidence supporting our risk-manager recommendation.

## M. PRACTICAL RISK MANAGEMENT

**Q: What should a risk manager do differently tomorrow?**
A: A risk manager analyzing horizon-specific trading strategies must recognize that tail dependence varies by timescale. However, when aggregating firm-wide risk, they must not linearly add independent horizon models. They must implement models that explicitly capture cross-horizon dependence, otherwise they will hold insufficient capital for severe crashes.

## N. LIMITATIONS

**Q: What is the weakest part of your model?**
A: The assumption that wavelet horizons can be simulated independently and summed. The SWT is a linear additive transform, which means returns sum perfectly. However, simulating from independent copulas at each horizon assumes that a short-term crash is uncorrelated with a long-term structural crash. In financial markets, extremes cluster across all frequencies simultaneously. This structural misspecification is the direct cause of the model's VaR underestimation.
